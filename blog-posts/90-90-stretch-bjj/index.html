The 90/90 Stretch for BJJ: Why It's the Most Important Mobility Drill You're Not Doing

What Is the 90/90?

The 90/90 hip stretch is exactly what it sounds like: a position where both legs are bent at 90 degrees — one in front of you, one behind you. The front leg is externally rotated at the hip, the back leg is internally rotated. You're effectively stretching both sides of the same joint simultaneously.

This makes the 90/90 unique. Most hip stretches only address one movement at a time. Pigeon hits external rotation. The couch stretch hits hip extension. But the 90/90? It challenges your hip to express external rotation on one side and internal rotation on the other at the same time — exactly what your hips do when you shrimp, when you play guard, and when you pass.

It's part of the complete hip mobility toolkit and for good reason. If I could only do one hip mobility drill for the rest of my BJJ career, this would be it.

Why BJJ Players Specifically Need It

BJJ creates a specific pattern of hip tightness that's different from most other athletes. Let me break it down.

External rotation loss. When you play closed guard, your legs are wrapped around your opponent, holding them in external rotation. You do this for rounds on end, for months, for years. Your hips adapt by becoming "stuck" in external rotation — they can't go back to neutral. The 90/90's back leg (the one behind you) is internally rotated, which directly counters this by pulling the hip back toward center.

Internal rotation loss. Sitting at a desk, sleeping in the fetal position, and driving all shorten the internal rotators. On the mats, this shows up as an inability to keep your knees aligned in side control or to generate power in your knee-on-belly. The 90/90's front leg (the one in front) is externally rotated, which opens the internal rotation side.

Asymmetry. Most BJJ players have a "good side" and a "bad side" for hip mobility. The 90/90 makes this painfully obvious. One side will feel significantly tighter than the other. That's the side you need to work on. The 90/90 doesn't let you hide your imbalances — it exposes them so you can fix them.

Back pain connection. Tight hips are a primary driver of lower back pain in BJJ. When your hips can't rotate, your lumbar spine takes the load. The 90/90 directly addresses the rotational restrictions that cause your lower back to compensate. If you have sciatica or a herniated disc, restoring hip rotation is often the missing piece.

"I've had BJJ players tell me their chronic lower back pain disappeared after two weeks of daily 90/90 work. Not because the stretch fixed their back — but because it freed their hips so their back could finally relax."

Step-by-Step Guide

Here's exactly how to set up the 90/90 so you get the benefit without hurting your knees.

🧘 The 90/90 Hip Stretch
Basic Setup
  1. Sit on the floor. Both legs bent to 90 degrees. Your front leg has the knee pointing forward and your shin parallel to the front edge of your mat. Your back leg points behind you, shin parallel to the back edge of your mat.
  2. Check for 90 degrees at the knee on both legs. If you feel any pinching in the knee joint, widen the angle slightly until the pinching disappears.
  3. Square your hips to face the front leg. Both sit bones should be on the floor. If one hip lifts, place a folded towel or yoga block under it.
  4. Lengthen your spine. Don't slouch. If you can't sit tall, you need to back off the stretch — you're too tight for this depth.
  5. Stay here for 5 deep breaths. Focus on relaxing the back hip (the one that feels tightest).
  6. Gently release and switch sides. Do not bounce or force.
⏱ 2–3 min per side

3 Progressions

Once the basic 90/90 becomes comfortable, progress through these three variations. Each one adds a different demand on your hips.

🧘 Progression #1
Basic 90/90 (Foundation)
  1. Set up as described above. Stay upright with a tall spine.
  2. Keep your hands on the floor beside you or on your thighs.
  3. Hold for 5 deep breaths. On each exhale, consciously relax the back hip.
  4. Goal: sit tall without hands for support for 30 seconds.
⏱ 2 min per side
🧘 Progression #2
Forward Lean (Increased External Rotation)
  1. Start in basic 90/90 setup.
  2. Walk your hands forward on the floor in front of you.
  3. Keep your spine straight — lead with your chest, not your head.
  4. Lean forward over the front leg. You'll feel the stretch intensify in the back hip (external rotation side).
  5. Go only to the point where you can still breathe deeply. If your breath shortens, you've gone too far.
  6. Hold for 5 breaths, then walk hands back and repeat 2 more times.
⏱ 2 min per side
🧘 Progression #3
Pancake 90/90 (Advanced Range)
  1. Start in basic 90/90 setup.
  2. Place both hands on the floor in front of you.
  3. Slowly walk your hands forward as far as you can while keeping your chest open and spine long.
  4. Lower your torso toward the floor between your legs. Imagine you're trying to lay your belly on the mat.
  5. This is an advanced variation. If you feel sharp pain in the back knee or hip, back off to the forward lean progression.
  6. Hold for 3–5 breaths. Walk hands back and come up slowly.
⏱ 1–2 min per side — only when basic and forward lean feel easy

The 90/90 Breathing Technique

Here's the secret that makes the 90/90 actually work: how you breathe determines how much you get out of this stretch.

Most people hold their breath in a deep hip stretch. This triggers the sympathetic nervous system (fight or flight), which tightens your muscles — exactly the opposite of what you want. The 90/90 demands that you breathe deliberately to override this reflex.

Here's the breathing pattern:

  1. Inhale through your nose for 4 counts. Expand your ribcage laterally (360-degree breathing). As you inhale, imagine your hip joint expanding.
  2. Hold at the top for 1 count. Briefly.
  3. Exhale through your mouth for 6 counts. Make the exhale longer than the inhale. This activates the parasympathetic nervous system (rest and digest). As you exhale, consciously relax the back hip — let it drop toward the floor.
  4. Repeat 5 times on each side. On the 4th and 5th exhale, you'll feel the hip release a little more.

This 4-1-6 breathing pattern is the key to deep hip opening. It's not about forcing the stretch — it's about breathing into the restriction and letting the nervous system release it.

How to Integrate Into Your Warmup

The 90/90 is not a "do it once and you're done" drill. To see real change, you need to do it consistently. Here's where to put it in your routine.

Before class (3 minutes): Do one round of the basic 90/90 on each side as part of your pre-training warmup. This wakes up the hip rotation before you start shrimping and guard drilling.

After class (5 minutes): The post-training cooldown is when your hips are warmest and most receptive. Do the forward lean progression (2 min each side) plus 1 minute of the pancake variation if you have the range.

Morning routine (10 minutes): Combine the 90/90 with other hip openers from the daily hip mobility routine. Morning is ideal because you're working against the stiffness from sleeping in the fetal position — the exact position that tightens the hips.

On off days (5 minutes): Do a single round on each side whenever you have a break. It's better to do 5 minutes daily than 30 minutes once a week.

If you're dealing with lower back pain or wondering whether you should stretch, start with just 2 minutes of basic 90/90 each side before class. See how your back responds. For most people, the relief is almost immediate.

Conclusion

The 90/90 hip stretch is not flashy. It won't impress anyone. But it's the single most effective tool I've found for restoring the hip rotation that BJJ steals from you.

Start with the basic setup. Breathe with the 4-1-6 pattern. Progress through the forward lean and pancake when you're ready. Do it daily — even if only for 2 minutes per side.

Your hips are the engine of your jiu-jitsu. The 90/90 keeps the engine running smoothly. Don't skip it.
