How Often Should You Train BJJ? The Recovery Math Nobody Talks About

Sebastian Brosche 2026-05-30 9 min read

Interviewer: Today we are talking with Sebastian Brosche, founder of Yoga for BJJ. Sebastian, the most common question from new students is: how often should I train? What is the real answer?

Sebastian: The real answer is: it depends on your recovery, not your ambition. Most people ask how often they can train. They should ask how often they can recover.

Interviewer: What do you mean by recovery?

Sebastian: Recovery is everything that happens between sessions. Sleep, nutrition, stress, mobility work, hydration. These factors determine how much training you can actually absorb. A 25-year-old who sleeps 8 hours, eats well, and has low stress can train 5 days a week. A 40-year-old with a stressful job, kids, and 6 hours of sleep cannot. Same sport, different capacity.

Interviewer: Is there a general guideline?

Sebastian: Yes, but it varies by experience level. Beginners : 0 to 2 years : should train 2 to 3 times per week. Their bodies are learning new movement patterns, and their connective tissues need time to adapt. More than 3 sessions and they risk overuse injuries. I see this constantly: enthusiastic white belts train 5 days a week, then they are out for a month with a shoulder or knee issue.

Interviewer: What about intermediate players?

Sebastian: 3 to 4 times per week for people with 2 to 5 years of experience. Their technique is solid, their body has adapted to the demands, and they can handle more volume. But they still need at least 2 rest days. Those rest days are when you get better. Your nervous system consolidates technique during rest. Your muscles repair during rest. Without rest, you are just accumulating fatigue.

Interviewer: And advanced players?

Sebastian: 4 to 5 times per week for 5+ years. But here is the key: intensity management. Advanced players should not go hard every session. Some days are for drilling and technique. Some days are for flow rolling. Only 1 to 2 days should be high-intensity competition training. I train 4 days a week at 41. Two days are technical, one day is moderate rolling, one day is hard. The other 3 days are mobility, strength work, and complete rest.

Interviewer: What are the warning signs of overtraining?

Sebastian: Persistent muscle soreness that does not resolve after 48 hours. Irritability and mood changes. Declining performance : techniques you normally hit stop working. Frequent minor injuries. Trouble sleeping despite fatigue. These are all signals that your training stress exceeds your recovery capacity.

Interviewer: What should someone do if they notice these signs?

Sebastian: Reduce volume immediately. Cut back by 30 to 40 percent for two weeks. Prioritize sleep. Add mobility work. Do not try to "push through it." Overtraining is not a character flaw. It is a physiological state that leads to injury and burnout. I have been there. It is not worth it.

Interviewer: How does age affect training frequency?

Sebastian: Recovery slows with age. A 20-year-old might need 24 hours to recover from a hard session. A 40-year-old might need 48 hours. This does not mean older athletes should train less. It means they need to be more deliberate about recovery. The actual training frequency might be similar, but the recovery work between sessions must increase.

Interviewer: What about people who can only train once or twice a week?

Sebastian: You can still improve. Consistency over time beats intensity. Someone who trains twice a week for 5 years will be better than someone who trains 5 times a week for 6 months then burns out. On limited days, focus on quality. Drill specifically. Ask questions. Take notes. And do solo work at home: hip escapes, bridges, mobility routines.

Interviewer: What is your current schedule?

Sebastian: Monday: BJJ technical training, 90 minutes. Tuesday: Mobility and strength, 45 minutes. Wednesday: BJJ rolling, 90 minutes. Thursday: Rest or light movement. Friday: BJJ drilling, 60 minutes. Saturday: Open mat, 2 hours. Sunday: Complete rest. This gives me 4 BJJ sessions, 1 strength session, 1 rest day, and 1 active recovery day.

Interviewer: Any final advice on training frequency?

Sebastian: Start with less than you think you need. Build up gradually. Pay attention to recovery signals. Remember that rest is training. Your body improves during recovery, not during the session. The goal is not to train as much as possible. The goal is to train as much as you can recover from.
