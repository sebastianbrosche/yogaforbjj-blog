Protecting Your Knees in BJJ: What Every Grappler Needs to Know

June 2026

Why BJJ Destroys Knees

Knee injuries are the single most common serious injury in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu. Heel hooks, knee bars, and twisted guard passes get the attention, but most knee problems in BJJ are not from a single catastrophic event. They are from accumulated stress, poor movement patterns, and a lack of preparation.

The BJJ knee is exposed to: rotational forces from guard play, lateral stress from takedowns, hyperextension risks from straight foot locks, and chronic compression from kneeling postures. Over years of training, these forces add up.

The Four Pillars of Knee Protection

1. Hip Mobility (The Knee's Best Friend)

Your knee is designed to bend and straighten in one plane. It does not enjoy twisting. When your hips are stiff, your knee compensates by absorbing rotational forces that should be handled by your hip joint.

Every guard pass, every sweep attempt, every scramble involves hip rotation. If your hips cannot provide that rotation, your ACL, MCL, and meniscus will.

The fix: The daily hip mobility routine from our guard retention article applies here too. Deep hip flexion and external rotation take load off your knees.

2. Ankle Mobility (The Foundation)

Your ankle is the first joint that should move when you change direction. If your ankle is stiff (common from years of wearing shoes with elevated heels), your knee absorbs the force instead.

The fix: Daily ankle dorsiflexion work — kneeling ankle stretches, deep squat holds, and calf releases with a lacrosse ball.

3. Quad and Hamstring Balance

Many BJJ athletes have disproportionately strong quads (from guard pulling, bridging, and shrimping) and weak hamstrings. This imbalance pulls the kneecap out of its natural tracking and increases ACL strain.

The fix: Add Nordic curls or glute-ham raises 2x per week. Single-leg RDLs also build hamstring strength while challenging balance.

4. Proprioception and Positional Awareness

Most knee injuries in BJJ happen when a grappler puts their knee in a vulnerable position without realising it. Examples: posting with a straight leg when someone dives on it, letting your knee cave inward during a takedown, or failing to recognise heel hook exposure.

The best protection is awareness. Know where your knees are. Keep them soft (slightly bent) when posting. Never let your knee collapse inward under load.

Mobilize Before You Roll

A 5-minute pre-roll knee prep:

  1. Leg swings (forward/back and side-to-side) — 30 seconds each
  2. Deep squat hold — 60 seconds
  3. Standing knee circles — 30 seconds each direction
  4. Lunges with rotation — 5 reps each side
  5. Single-leg balance with eyes closed — 30 seconds each side

Listen to Your Knees

Your knees give feedback before they break. A dull ache during deep flexion, a pinch during rotation, or a weird pop during warm-up are all warning signs. Do not train through them. Back off, mobilise, and address the movement pattern that caused it.

The grapplers who train for 30+ years are not the ones who ignored their knees. They are the ones who respected them.
