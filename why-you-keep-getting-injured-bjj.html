Why You Keep Getting Injured in BJJ (10 Fixable Reasons)

Sebastian Brosche 2026-09-13 14 min read

Jiu jitsu can change your life if it does not break you first. The white belt who disappears after one bad injury, the blue belt with tape on every finger, the purple belt limping through warm-ups: that is treated as normal. It is a pattern. Patterns can be changed.

This is the injuries ebook as a pillar. Each reason is something you can actually do something about. Injuries are not accidents. They are habits wearing a gi.

1. You think warm-ups do not matter

Most BJJ injuries do not wait for round ten. They happen when you are cold: a rib in a scramble, a knee on a post, a back in guard retention. Gym warm-ups that are jogging and push-ups elevate heart rate and skip the spine, neck, and knees.

You need full-body joint prep, pattern priming for that day's technique, and a mental shift so you are loose and present, not fried. Fifteen minutes should be the standard. If class will not give it, do it yourself.

Link: How to warm up for BJJ

2. You only move when you train

You spend most of the day sitting, then you ask your hips to invert. The body adapts to what you do often. Hunched and passive becomes the default. Then a shrimp or a post hits tissue that never saw that range.

Off-mat mobility is not foam rolling as a personality. It is 10 to 15 minutes of slow, guided, floor-based movement: decompress the spine, restore hips and shoulders, build control in the positions you roll in. Brush your joints or pay in time off.

Link: Mobility for BJJ

3. You are not strong enough to survive your own mistakes

Grappling builds skill and timing. It does not automatically build dense bone or tendons that can take a bad angle. Technique will not save you if the tissue folds when someone lands on you wrong.

One heavy-ish session a week (squat, hinge, press, pull) plus one lighter home session is enough for most hobbyists. After 30, unused muscle and bone decline. The goal is not shredded. The goal is a body that can absorb chaos.

4. You only train your A-game

Same guard, same side, same pass. It works, so you repeat it, and you build a body that only knows one corridor. The other hip never rotates. The other shoulder never learns. Then sparring finds the weak side.

Train both sides. Play top half the time even if you love pulling guard. Start in bad positions on purpose. Variation is injury prevention, not only a strategy for medals.

5. You train like an athlete and live like a teenager

Hard rounds, then phone in bed, leftover takeout, low-grade burnout. Sleep under seven hours slows tissue healing and raises pain sensitivity. Most people think they eat well. Far fewer actually do. Stress loads the system the same way training does. The "small" scramble is just the last straw.

Turn the screen off earlier. Eat like you want the body for 40 more years. That is not motivation talk. It is matching your life to the sport you already chose.

6. You do not know what recovery means

A day on the couch is rest. It is not recovery for a grappler who needs to train again in 24 to 48 hours. You walk off the mat inflamed and braced. Sleep helps. It is not the whole protocol.

Active recovery as I teach it in PEARS: mobility, long stretching, breathwork, NSDR. Contrast, not a lighter version of the same drilling. If you are over 30 and still treating recovery as optional, biology will bill you.

Link: BJJ recovery blueprint

7. You train to get tough

Roger Gracie, talking to Lex Fridman: most people do not get better because they train to get tough, not to get better. You have to practice the weakness.

Tough enough to never tap, never say no, 100 percent every round: that thought wrecks more bodies than "I am technical" ever will. Toughness, if it shows up, is a side effect of getting good and staying healthy.

8. You skip the early warning signs

(Chapter 10 in the ebook plan.) The tweak you train through, the night of sleep you keep shortening, the partner you know is chaotic. Your body sends the memo. Ego deletes it. The next injury feels sudden. It was not.

9. You train at the wrong gym

Culture drives injury rates. Reckless rolling, instructors who mock tapping, a room that runs on shame. You can do everything else on this list and still get broken by the person who treats your knee as a puzzle they have not finished.

Leave, or change class times, before you "tough it out" into surgery. Principle 9 in Do BJJ for Life is the same point from the longevity side.

10. You have no plan

The intro of the ebook is the tenth reason. Crazy partners, useless warm-ups, hardcore culture, no body awareness, no mobility outside the gym: people show up and try to tough it out. In a chaotic sport that ends the same way.

The plan is this page, the warm-up, the off-mat 15 minutes, the recovery hour, and the ego rules. Free PDFs exist because I got tired of watching people I like limp out of the sport they love.
