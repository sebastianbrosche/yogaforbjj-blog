Anterior Pelvic Tilt in BJJ: The Postural Problem Every Grappler Has

By Sebastian Brosche 2026-06-10 · 9 min read

Look at any photo of BJJ players on the mats. Notice that arched lower back — the way the pelvis tilts forward and the belly drops down. Most grapplers think this is normal. It's not. It's anterior pelvic tilt, and it's the hidden cause of most BJJ lower back pain, tight hip flexors, and weak glute activation.

What Is Anterior Pelvic Tilt?

Anterior pelvic tilt (APT) is when the front of your pelvis drops down and forward, and the back rises up. This creates an exaggerated arch in your lower back, pushes your belly forward, and shifts your weight onto your heels.

In BJJ, APT is almost universal. The combination of sitting in guard (hip flexion), shrimping (hip extension from a flexed position), and bridging (forced lumbar extension) creates a pattern where the hip flexors get tight and the glutes stop firing.

How APT Hurts Your BJJ

  • Lower back pain — The lumbar spine is in constant extension, compressing the facet joints and loading the posterior discs.
  • Weak bridge — If your glutes aren't firing, your hamstrings and lower back do all the work. Your bridge is weaker and you'll strain your back.
  • Poor guard retention — APT limits hip internal rotation, making it harder to shrimp and recover guard.
  • Tight hip flexors — The psoas is in a shortened position. This limits hip extension and makes passing difficult.
  • Shallow breathing — APT compresses the diaphragm, limiting your ability to breathe deeply during intense rolls.

The 10-Minute APT Fix

1. Couch Stretch (Hip Flexor Release) — 2 min per side

Kneel facing a couch or wall. Place your back foot up against the wall, shin vertical. Sink your hips forward. Keep your torso tall. Breathe deeply. This releases the psoas and rectus femoris — the two muscles pulling your pelvis forward.

2. Glute Bridge (Wake Up the Glutes) — 3x15

Lie on your back, knees bent. Drive through your heels, squeeze your glutes, and lift your hips. Hold 2 seconds at the top. The goal is to feel this in your glutes — if you feel it in your lower back, you're compensating.

3. Dead Bug (Core Reset) — 3x8 per side

APT is partly a core stability problem. The dead bug teaches your deep core to stabilize your pelvis in a neutral position. This is the position your pelvis should be in during BJJ — not tucked, not arched, neutral.

4. Supine 90/90 Breathing — 2 minutes

Lie on your back, hips and knees at 90 degrees, feet on a wall or chair. Place one hand on your belly, one on your chest. Breathe deeply into your belly for 2 minutes. This trains diaphragmatic breathing, which is the foundation of core stability.
