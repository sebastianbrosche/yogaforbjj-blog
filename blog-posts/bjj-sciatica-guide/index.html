What Sciatica Actually Is (And What It Isn't)

Let's clear something up right away: sciatica is not a diagnosis. It's a symptom. It describes pain that follows the path of the sciatic nerve — the largest nerve in your body, running from your lower back through your glutes, down the back of your leg, and into your foot.

When BJJ players say they have sciatica, what they usually mean is: "I have a sharp, shooting, or burning pain in my lower back, glute, and/or leg that gets worse when I train." That pain can come from several different sources:

  • Disc-related: A herniated or bulging disc in the lower back presses on the nerve root before it forms the sciatic nerve. This is the most common cause and the most serious. The pain usually starts in the lower back and radiates down the leg.
  • Piriformis-related: The piriformis muscle (a small muscle deep in your glute) spasms and compresses the sciatic nerve as it passes through or under the muscle. This is often mistaken for disc issues. The pain is typically more localized in the glute and may not involve the lower back at all.
  • Joint-related: Issues in the sacroiliac (SI) joint can refer pain along the sciatic nerve pathway. This is common in BJJ due to the asymmetrical loading of the hips in guard positions.

Why does this matter? Because the treatment is different for each cause. Stretching your hamstrings won't help a disc issue. Rolling a lacrosse ball on your glute won't fix an SI joint problem. You need to know what you're dealing with.

"Sciatica is not a tight muscle you can stretch away. It's a nerve being compressed — and stretching an already compressed nerve is like pulling on a frayed rope."

The 5 BJJ Positions That Flare Up Sciatica

Through personal experience and conversations with dozens of BJJ players, I've identified the five most common positional triggers for sciatic nerve pain. If you have sciatica, pay close attention to how these feel.

1. Deep Half Guard (Bottom)

The deep half guard position requires you to curl your body under your opponent, with your head and shoulders on one side of their body and your hips on the other. This creates a sustained stretch through the glute and piriformis on the side of the trapped leg — exactly where the sciatic nerve runs. Fix: If deep half triggers your sciatica, try playing knee shield or Z-guard instead. You'll maintain a safer angle for your hip.

2. Berimbolo and Inversion Entries

Any position where you invert or put your hips over your head places extreme tension on the sciatic nerve. The nerve has to stretch across multiple joints (hip, knee, ankle) while under load. Fix: Avoid inverting entirely if you have active sciatica. There are plenty of sweeps and guard passes that don't require inversion.

3. Butterfly Guard (Sustained)

Sitting in butterfly guard with your legs hooked under your opponent's thighs creates a position where your hips are flexed and externally rotated. Over 15-20 minutes of drilling or rolling, this sustained position can compress the sciatic nerve against the piriformis. Fix: Get up and reset frequently. Don't sit in butterfly for extended periods. Use stand-ups and resets to break the sustained position.

4. Stacked Pass Drills

When your opponent stacks you — driving your knees toward your chest — the sciatic nerve gets stretched from both ends. The hip flexes and the knee extends, creating tension along the entire nerve pathway. This is a recipe for sciatic flare-ups. Fix: Tap early to stacks. Don't let your training partner fold you in half. Frame on their hips and create space early.

5. 50/50 Guard

The 50/50 guard (both players have one leg entangled) creates asymmetrical tension in the hips. The external rotation and flexion of the trapped leg can compress the sciatic nerve. This position also makes it hard to escape, meaning you're stuck in the aggravating position longer. Fix: If 50/50 triggers your sciatica, focus on exiting early — don't wait to try a sweep from there.

The 3 Stretches That Work

Most "sciatica stretches" you find online are worthless — or worse, they make things worse. The classic sit-and-reach hamstring stretch actually increases tension on the sciatic nerve. These three stretches are different. They work because they address the nerve and the muscle compression, not just the muscle.

1. Pigeon With a Twist

Standard pigeon pose can trigger sciatica for some people because the forward fold compresses the nerve. This modified version keeps the spine upright and adds a twist for the piriformis.

🧘 Sciatica Relief Stretch
Pigeon With a Twist
  1. Start in a tabletop position. Bring your right knee forward toward your right wrist, and place your right ankle near your left hip.
  2. Keep your left leg extended straight back behind you. Keep the top of your left foot on the mat.
  3. Instead of folding forward (which can compress the nerve), keep your torso upright — stack your shoulders over your hips.
  4. Place your right hand on your right knee and your left hand on the mat behind you.
  5. Slowly twist your torso to the right, looking over your right shoulder.
  6. Breathe deeply for 5 breaths. You should feel this in the right glute and outer hip — not shooting down your leg.
  7. Switch sides and repeat.
⏱ 5 breaths each side — do twice daily

2. Seated Figure-4 Stretch

This targets the piriformis without placing the spine in flexion. It's safer than the lying figure-4 (sometimes called the "figure-4 stretch" or "piriformis stretch") because it keeps your spine upright and neutral.

🧘 Piriformis Release
Seated Figure-4
  1. Sit on a chair or bench with your feet flat on the floor. Keep your spine tall — do not slouch.
  2. Cross your right ankle over your left knee, creating a figure-4 shape with your legs.
  3. Keep your right foot flexed to protect the knee joint.
  4. Gently press down on your right knee, feeling a stretch in the right glute and outer hip.
  5. To increase the stretch, hinge forward slightly from the hips (not the lower back), keeping your spine long.
  6. Hold for 30 seconds, breathing deeply. Switch sides.
  7. Do 3 rounds on each side.
⏱ 3 min total — do before and after training

3. Sciatic Nerve Glides (Flossing)

This is the most important exercise in this article. Nerve glides (also called nerve flossing) help the sciatic nerve move freely through its surrounding tissues. When the nerve is compressed (by a disc, muscle, or joint), it becomes "sticky" and can't glide properly. These exercises restore its range of motion.

🫀 Nerve Mobility
Sciatic Nerve Glide (Flossing)
  1. Lie on your back with both knees bent, feet flat on the floor.
  2. Lift your right leg into a tabletop position (knee bent 90°, shin parallel to floor).
  3. Hold behind your right thigh with both hands.
  4. Slowly extend your right knee until you feel a gentle stretch, then immediately bend it back. Do NOT force into pain — you should feel a mild sensation, not sharp pain.
  5. As you extend your knee, flex your foot (bring your toes toward your shin). As you bend your knee, point your foot.
  6. Move slowly and rhythmically: extend (flex foot) — hold 2 seconds — bend (point foot).
  7. Repeat 10-15 times. The sensation should move from your glute down your hamstring and back.
  8. Switch legs.
⏱ 2 min per leg — do before every class

Important: Nerve glides should feel like a gentle stretch or "nerve tingle," not sharp pain. If it hurts, reduce the range of motion. Never force a nerve glide.

How to Modify Your Game

If you have sciatica, your BJJ game needs to change — temporarily or permanently. Here's what I recommend based on what worked for me and dozens of other grapplers:

Play Top, Not Bottom

Bottom positions require more hip flexion, which compresses the sciatic nerve. Top positions (mount, side control, knee-on-belly) put your hips in extension, which relieves nerve tension. If you have active sciatica, focus on takedowns and passing. Let your partner start on bottom.

Avoid Inverted Positions

Just don't invert. It's that simple. If you play guard, stick to positions that keep your hips below your shoulders: closed guard, half guard (knee shield), butterfly (with frequent resets), and single-leg X.

Use Knee Shield Instead of Deep Half

The knee shield position (your knee pressing into your opponent's bicep/chest) keeps your hip in a more neutral position than deep half guard. If deep half triggers your sciatica, use the knee shield as your primary half guard entry for 4-6 weeks.

Prioritize Flow Rolling

Flow rolling at 40-50% intensity allows you to maintain the movement patterns of BJJ without the explosive, high-tension positions that trigger nerve pain. Do flow rolling for 70% of your rounds. Save the hard rounds for when your symptoms are minimal.

When You CAN Train vs When You SHOULDN'T

This is the question every BJJ player with sciatica asks. Here's my framework:

GREEN LIGHT — Train (With Modifications)

  • The pain is a dull ache or tightness in the glute, without leg radiation.
  • The pain is bilateral (both sides) or symmetrical.
  • You can do the nerve glide test (above) without sharp pain.
  • Movement makes it feel better, not worse.

YELLOW LIGHT — Light Training Only

  • The pain radiates down the leg but stops above the knee.
  • You have some numbness or tingling in the foot.
  • Certain positions (but not all) trigger the pain.
  • Action: Drill techniques, do positional sparring at 30%, avoid trigger positions. Do nerve glides after class.

RED LIGHT — Do Not Train

  • Sharp, shooting, or electric pain down the leg.
  • Foot drop (difficulty lifting the front of your foot).
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control (seek medical attention immediately).
  • Pain that wakes you up at night.
  • Pain that persists for more than 2 weeks despite modifying training.

If you're in the red zone, take 7-10 days completely off BJJ. Do nerve glides, the seated figure-4 stretch, and gentle walking. See a physiotherapist who understands BJJ. Most sciatica episodes resolve with conservative care within 2-4 weeks if you stop aggravating the nerve.

The 60-Second Nerve Flossing Routine Before Every Class

Here's a routine I do before every single BJJ class when my sciatica is acting up. It takes 60 seconds and dramatically reduces my risk of a flare-up during training.

⚡ Pre-Class Nerve Prep
60-Second Sciatica Prevention Routine
  1. Seated Figure-4 (20 sec each side): On the edge of the mat or a chair, cross one ankle over the opposite knee. Gently press the knee down. Breathe.
  2. Supine Nerve Glide (10 reps each side): Lie on your back. Hold one leg behind the thigh and gently extend/flex the knee while moving the foot. This "flosses" the nerve before class.
  3. Cat-Cow with Hip Circles (5 reps): On hands and knees, circle your hips gently in both directions. This mobilizes the SI joint and piriformis.
  4. Standing Hamstring Nerve Desensitization (5 reps each side): Place one foot on a low mat or step. Keep the knee slightly bent. Gently flex and point the foot while keeping the spine neutral. Do not round your back to reach for your toes.
⏱ 60 sec total — do before every class when symptomatic

I've been doing this routine for over a year. Before I started, I'd get sciatic flare-ups every 2-3 weeks that would keep me off the mats for days. Now I get maybe one flare-up every 3 months, and it's usually mild enough to train through (with modifications).

Sciatica is annoying, frustrating, and sometimes scary. But it doesn't have to end your BJJ journey. Understand what's causing it, avoid the positions that trigger it, do the right stretches (not the wrong ones), and modify your game. You can keep training.
