The Myth: "Stretch What Hurts"

It makes intuitive sense, right? Your lower back feels tight after BJJ class. It aches when you sit at your desk. So you bend forward, grab your toes, and try to stretch it out. Maybe you sit on the mat and pull your knees to your chest, or you lean over sideways while sitting in a chair.

I did exactly this for years. And for years, my back kept getting worse.

The problem is that "stretch what hurts" is one of the most pervasive and dangerous myths in fitness and BJJ. It's wrong — often catastrophically wrong — for the most common causes of lower back pain in grapplers.

In this article, I'm going to explain why, give you a simple test to determine if you should stretch or strengthen, and show you exactly what to do instead. I'm a yoga instructor and a purple belt who has dealt with chronic back pain for years. I've made every mistake in the book so you don't have to.

"The most common cause of lower back pain isn't tightness — it's your body desperately trying to stabilize an unstable joint. Stretching it is like loosening the bolts on a wobbly chair."

Why Stretching a Painful Lower Back Makes It Worse

To understand this, you need to understand what's actually happening when your lower back hurts after BJJ. It's almost never what you think.

The Real Cause: Stability, Not Tightness

When your lower back feels tight after training, it's usually not because your back muscles are short and need to be lengthened. It's because your deep spinal stabilizers (multifidus, transversus abdominis) are fatigued or inhibited, and your superficial erector spinae muscles are clamping down to compensate. The "tightness" is a protective spasm — your body's way of saying, "I don't trust your deep stabilizers, so I'm going to lock everything down to prevent injury."

When you stretch these muscles — especially with forward bending or passive flexion — you're overriding your body's protective mechanism. You may feel temporary relief (stretching feels good, I know), but you're actually telling your nervous system: "See? I can move even though nothing's stable." The next time you train, your body will compensate even harder, creating a cycle of tightness → stretching → more instability → more tightness.

The Disc Danger

For anyone with disc issues (bulging, herniated, or degenerative discs), forward bending stretches are actively dangerous. When you bend forward, you compress the front of the disc and force the nucleus pulposus backward. If your disc is already compromised, this backward pressure can push the disc material further into the nerve root. This is how a manageable back issue becomes a surgical one.

Every year, I meet BJJ players who made their disc herniation worse by doing "toe touches" or "knees to chest" stretches to relieve their back pain. Don't be one of them.

The Hamstring Connection

Tight hamstrings are often blamed for lower back pain, and the solution is usually "stretch your hamstrings." But here's the problem: if your hamstrings are genuinely tight, it's often because your body has adapted to protect your back. Short, strong hamstrings reduce the movement demand on your lower back. Stretching them aggressively — especially with straight-leg forward folds — can actually destabilize your pelvis and increase the load on your lumbar spine.

The 3-Minute Test: Should You Stretch or Strengthen?

Here's a simple test you can do right now. It takes 3 minutes and will tell you whether your back pain needs stretching or strengthening.

🔬 Self-Assessment
The 3-Minute Stretch vs. Strengthen Test
  1. Step 1 (30 sec): Stand up and notice your back pain. Rate it 0-10.
  2. Step 2 (60 sec): Lie on your back with knees bent, feet flat on the floor. Place your hands on your belly. Breathe deeply into your belly for 60 seconds — let your ribs and belly expand in all directions. Don't force anything.
  3. Step 3 (30 sec): While still on your back, gently press your lower back into the floor. Engage your deep core (think "zip up a tight jeans zipper"). Hold for 5 seconds, release. Repeat 3 times.
  4. Step 4 (60 sec): Stand up again. Notice your back pain. Rate it 0-10.
⏱ 3 min total

What Your Results Mean

  • Pain decreased (by 2+ points): You likely need stability and core engagement. Your body is craving support, not stretch. Proceed to the strengthening exercises below.
  • Pain stayed the same: You may have a hip mobility issue. Your lower back is compensating for tight hips. Proceed to the hip mobilization exercises.
  • Pain increased: Stop this test. You may have an acute injury or nerve involvement. See a physiotherapist before doing any further exercises.

I've done this test with dozens of BJJ players at my gym. Over 80% of them fall into the "pain decreased" category — meaning they need stability, not stretching. Almost none of them actually need to stretch their lower back.

What to Do Instead: Mobilize the Hips, Not the Spine

Here's the fundamental principle that changed everything for me: your lower back hurts because your hips aren't doing their job.

The hip joints are designed for large ranges of motion — flexion, extension, rotation, circumduction. The lumbar spine is designed for stability — small, controlled movements with strength. When your hips lose mobility (which happens in BJJ due to all the time spent in guard, sitting, and in flexed positions), your lower back has to compensate by moving more than it should. That compensation creates irritation, inflammation, and eventually pain.

The solution is not to stretch your lower back. It's to restore mobility to your hips so your lower back can go back to doing what it does best: being stable.

This shift in thinking — from "my back is tight" to "my hips are tight and my back is compensating" — is the single most important mindset change you can make for long-term back health in BJJ.

Exercises That Actually Help

Here are the exercises I recommend to every BJJ player with lower back pain. They target the actual source of the problem: the hips and the deep core.

1. Hip Flexor Release

Tight hip flexors pull your pelvis into anterior tilt, which compresses your lower back. This is the most common postural problem in BJJ players.

🦵 Hip Mobilization
Half-Kneeling Hip Flexor Release
  1. Kneel on your left knee, with your right foot flat on the floor in front of you (lunge position).
  2. Tuck your pelvis under — imagine you're trying to close a gap between your ribs and your hips.
  3. Keep your torso upright and your core engaged. Do NOT arch your lower back.
  4. Gently shift your weight forward until you feel a stretch in the front of your left hip.
  5. Raise your left arm overhead and slightly lean to the right to intensify the stretch.
  6. Breathe deeply into your belly for 45 seconds. Switch sides.
⏱ 45 sec each side — do twice daily

2. Glute Activation

Your glutes are your back's best friend. When they're weak or inhibited, your lower back takes over hip extension during BJJ — every bridge, every hip escape, every takedown. Over time, this overworks your spinal erectors and creates chronic tightness and pain.

💪 Glute Activation
Single-Leg Glute Bridge
  1. Lie on your back with knees bent, feet flat on the floor, arms by your sides.
  2. Extend your right leg straight up toward the ceiling.
  3. Press through your left heel and lift your hips toward the ceiling. Squeeze your left glute at the top.
  4. Keep your hips level — don't let them drop to one side.
  5. Lower your hips with control. Do not let them touch the floor between reps.
  6. Do 12-15 reps on each leg. If this is too hard, start with both feet on the floor (standard glute bridge).
⏱ 3 min total — do daily

3. Core Bracing (Dead Bug)

Dead bugs train your deep core to stabilize your spine while your limbs move. This is exactly what your back needs during BJJ — it needs to stay still and stable while everything else moves around it.

🪲 Core Stabilization
Dead Bug — Anti-Extension Core Drill
  1. Lie on your back with arms extended toward the ceiling and knees bent at 90 degrees.
  2. Press your lower back firmly into the mat. Imagine you're holding a piece of paper under your back — don't let it move.
  3. Slowly extend your right arm overhead and your left leg toward the floor simultaneously.
  4. Keep your lower back pressed into the mat. If it lifts, stop — you've gone too far.
  5. Return to center and switch sides.
  6. Breathe steadily. Do 8 controlled reps per side.
⏱ 3 min — do daily, especially before BJJ

The ONE Stretch That's Safe When Your Back Is in Acute Pain

There is exactly one stretch I recommend when your lower back is in the acute phase — meaning you can barely move, it hurts to sit, and you're wondering if you should even go to class.

It's not a back stretch. It's a ribcage stretch.

🧘 Safe Acute-Phase Stretch
Supine Ribcage Mobility (The Only Safe Stretch)
  1. Lie on your back with knees bent, feet flat on the floor, arms by your sides.
  2. Place your hands on the sides of your ribcage, thumbs pointing up toward your armpits.
  3. Inhale slowly through your nose for 4 seconds. Feel your ribcage expand in all directions — forward, backward, and sideways — into your hands.
  4. Exhale slowly through your mouth for 6 seconds. Feel your ribcage narrow and your ribs drop toward your pelvis.
  5. On your exhale, gently imagine your lower back relaxing into the floor. Don't force it — just allow it.
  6. Continue for 10-15 breaths. Do not move your spine. The only movement is your ribcage expanding and contracting with your breath.
⏱ 3 min — safe even in acute pain

Why does this work? Because when your back is in acute pain, your nervous system is in a heightened state of protection. Forcing a stretch will increase that protective response. But breath-focused ribcage mobility activates your parasympathetic nervous system (the rest-and-digest response), which downregulates pain signals. It also improves the mechanics of your diaphragm, which is directly connected to your spinal stability system.

I've used this technique to go from "can't stand up straight" to "on the mat drilling technique" in 15 minutes. It's not a cure — but it's the safest thing you can do when stretching would make things worse.

When to See a Professional

Not all back pain can be solved with mobility and core work. Here are the signs that you need to see a physiotherapist, chiropractor, or sports medicine doctor — ideally one who understands BJJ:

  • Radiating pain — Pain that travels down one leg, especially past the knee. This suggests nerve involvement (sciatica, disc herniation).
  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness — Especially in the legs or feet. This is a neurological sign that needs professional assessment.
  • Loss of bladder or bowel control — This is a medical emergency (cauda equina syndrome). Go to the ER immediately.
  • Pain lasting more than 6 weeks — Acute pain becomes chronic pain at the 6-8 week mark. Early intervention prevents chronicity.
  • Pain that wakes you up at night — Mechanical back pain usually feels better when lying down. If it wakes you up, there may be an inflammatory or systemic component.
  • Pain that doesn't change with position — Mechanical pain changes when you move (gets better or worse). Constant pain regardless of position may indicate something other than a musculoskeletal issue.

I see a physiotherapist every 3-4 months even when I'm not in pain. It's like getting your alignment checked on your car — much cheaper than waiting for something to break. Find a physio who treats BJJ players or at least understands the demands of the sport.

"The best back pain treatment is the one you do before the pain starts. Daily hip mobility and core stability will prevent more back pain than any stretch ever could."

Next time your lower back hurts after BJJ, resist the urge to bend forward and grab your toes. Lie on your back, breathe into your ribcage, and ask yourself: do my hips need mobility? Do my glutes need activation? Does my core need stability? The answer is almost always yes to one of these — and almost never "I need to stretch my back."

Summary: Don't stretch your lower back when it hurts — you're probably overriding a protective mechanism or worsening a disc issue. Use the 3-minute test to determine if you need stability, hip mobility, or professional care. Do hip flexor releases, glute activation, and dead bugs instead of back stretches. The one safe stretch during acute pain is supine ribcage breathing. See a professional if you have radiating pain, numbness, night pain, or symptoms lasting more than 6 weeks.
