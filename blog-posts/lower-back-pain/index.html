Introduction: The Problem

Let's be honest with each other. BJJ destroys backs.

I don't say that to scare you. I say it because it's true — and pretending otherwise is why so many people quit the sport they love. The hunched postures, the explosive hip escapes, the heavy pressure from top side control, the stacking in half guard — every single one of these puts stress on your lumbar spine.

I know this because I've lived it. I'm a purple belt, I've been training since my late 30s, and I have a herniated disc at L4-L5. I've spent years figuring out what works and what doesn't when it comes to training BJJ with lower back pain. This guide is everything I wish someone had told me when I started.

The good news: you absolutely can train BJJ with lower back pain. Thousands of people do it. The key is not to ignore the pain — it's to train smarter. These 7 rules are the framework I've used to stay on the mats for years without making my back worse.

"The back doesn't break from one big injury. It breaks from a thousand small ignorances repeated over years."

Rule 1: Warm Up Your Spine Before Class

The single biggest mistake I see BJJ players with back pain making: walking onto the mat cold and expecting their spine to handle a warmup that includes shrimp drills and forward rolls.

Your spinal discs are filled with a gel-like nucleus that needs to be hydrated and moving before it can handle load. When you wake up, your discs are actually more fluid-filled and more vulnerable to injury. Here's my pre-class warmup. It takes 5 minutes. Do it before you even step on the mat.

🧘 Pre-Class Spine Warmup
5-Minute Sequence (Do This Before Every Class)
  1. Cat-Cow (10 reps): On hands and knees. Inhale, drop belly, lift chest and tailbone (cow). Exhale, round spine, tuck chin (cat). One breath per movement. Pumps fluid into spinal discs.
  2. Thread the Needle (30 sec each side): From hands and knees, slide right arm under left, lowering right shoulder and ear to mat. Breathe into ribcage. Mobilizes thoracic spine.
  3. 90/90 Breathing (10 breaths each side): Sit with one leg bent 90° in front and the other 90° behind. Hands on ribcage. Breathe deep into belly and back. Resets diaphragm, takes pressure off lower back.
  4. World's Greatest Stretch (3 breaths each side): From a lunge, drop back knee. Twist torso toward front leg, reaching arm up. Opens hip flexors and thoracic spine.
⏱ 5 min total — do before every class

Rule 2: Choose Your Training Partners Carefully

This is the hardest rule to follow because it requires social awkwardness. But it's also the most important.

Not every training partner is safe for someone with lower back pain. The 240-pound white belt who spazzes from bottom side control and explodes into a reversal? A back injury waiting to happen. The competition-focused blue belt who insists on maximum pressure from top? Hard pass.

  • Have the conversation before you roll. "Hey, I have a back injury. Can we flow at about 60%?" 9 out of 10 people will say yes.
  • Seek out upper belts. Purple belts and above generally have better body awareness and are less likely to spazz.
  • Find your "safe" partners. Identify 3-4 people who roll in a way that doesn't aggravate your back. Prioritize them.
  • Say no. You are never obligated to roll with anyone. "I'm taking this round off" is a complete sentence.

I've turned down rolls with world-class competitors because I knew their style would wreck my back. Awkward for 10 seconds. A herniated disc is awkward for months. Choose wisely.

Rule 3: Avoid These 5 Positions

There are specific positions in BJJ that are disproportionately dangerous for people with lower back pain. You don't need to avoid BJJ — you need to avoid these positions.

1. Stacked in Half Guard

When your opponent smashes your knees toward your chest, your lumbar spine compresses. This is the single most common position that aggravates my back. Solution: Frame on their hip, get to your side, work for the underhook before they settle the weight.

2. Heavy Pressure from Top Side Control

Being pinned under someone who drops their weight while you bridge creates shearing force on the lower back. Solution: Frame with your arms — don't bench-press them off. Use the elbow-knee connection to create space.

3. Getting Stacked in Closed Guard (Opponent Standing)

When your opponent stands up and drives forward, your lower back bears the brunt. Solution: Open your guard early if you feel them standing. Go to butterfly or single-leg X.

4. Deep Half Guard Bottom

Sustained spinal flexion under load can aggravate disc issues. Solution: Keep your head lifted and spine neutral. Don't let your chin drop to your chest.

5. Berimbolo Attempts

The inverted nature of the berimbolo places extreme loads on the lumbar spine. Even defending it is problematic. Solution: Concede the pass and work from a different position. Your back is worth more than sweep points.

Rule 4: Tap Early on Back Attacks

When someone attacks your back — whether it's a body triangle or a bow-and-arrow setup — tap early. The damage to your back comes from the position itself, not the choke. When someone locks a body triangle, the rotational torque on your lumbar spine can be significant.

My rule: if someone gets to my back and locks any kind of body triangle or figure-four, I tap. Not because I'm choking. Because my back matters more than that round.

"The smartest tap is the one you do before the damage. Not after."

Rule 5: Cool Down Is Non-Negotiable

Your back is most vulnerable 30 minutes after training. Muscles fatigued, discs loaded, nervous system in fight-or-flight. Skip the cooldown and you'll wake up barely able to get out of bed. Here's what I do after every single class:

🧘 Post-Class Cooldown
5-Minute Routine (Do This After Every Class)
  1. Child's Pose (10 breaths): Kneel, big toes together, sit back on heels, extend arms forward. Widen knees if hips are tight. Breathe into lower back. Decompresses lumbar spine.
  2. Piriformis Stretch (45 sec each side): Lie on back, cross one ankle over opposite knee, pull bottom leg toward chest. Feel in glute, not back.
  3. Supine Twist (45 sec each side): Lie on back with knees bent. Drop both knees to one side. Keep shoulders flat. Mobilizes thoracic spine.
  4. Knees-to-Chest Hug (30 sec): Pull both knees into chest, rock side to side. Releases psoas and hip flexors.
  5. Constructive Rest (2 min): Lie on back with knees bent, feet flat, hands on belly. Breathe naturally. Let nervous system settle.
⏱ 5 min total — non-negotiable after every session

Rule 6: Do 10 Minutes of Targeted Mobility Every Morning

BJJ doesn't injure your back in one session. It accumulates. The solution is a daily mobility practice that specifically addresses the patterns BJJ creates:

  • Hip flexors: BJJ keeps hips in flexion. Tight hip flexors pull pelvis forward, compressing lower back. Fix: 2 min half-kneeling hip flexor stretch daily.
  • Thoracic spine: BJJ rounds the upper back. A stiff T-spine forces lower back to compensate. Fix: 2 min thoracic rotations and open books daily.
  • Glutes: Weak glutes mean lower back takes over hip extension. Fix: 2 min glute bridges daily.
  • Deep core: Weak TVA means spine lacks stability. Fix: 2 min dead bugs or bird dogs.

I do this in the morning before getting out of bed. It takes 10 minutes. It has kept me training for years.

Rule 7: Know the Difference Between Pain and Soreness

If you can't distinguish between "this is uncomfortable" and "this is damaging," you will either quit BJJ unnecessarily or injure yourself permanently.

Soreness: Dull ache in lower back muscles. Feels like you did 100 good mornings. Gets better with movement. Symmetrical. Peaks 24-48 hours after training.

Pain (the bad kind): Sharp, stabbing, or burning. Often one-sided. Radiates into glute or leg. Worsens with forward bending. Feels electrical. This is nerve-related.

My rule: if it's muscular and bilateral, I train. If sharp, one-sided, or radiating, I take the day off. If persists more than 2 days, I see my physio.

The 5-Minute Daily Routine

Here's a routine you can do anywhere — at home, in the gym parking lot before class, or during lunch. It targets the three most common problem areas: tight hips, stiff upper back, weak core.

⚡ Daily Back Maintenance Routine
Do This Every Day (5 Minutes Total)
  1. Cat-Cow (30 sec / 5 reps): On hands and knees, cycle through cat and cow with your breath.
  2. Half-Kneeling Hip Flexor Stretch (60 sec each side): Lunge position with back knee down. Tuck pelvis under, lean forward. Don't arch the lower back.
  3. Thoracic Open Book (45 sec each side): Lie on side with knees bent. Open top arm like opening a book. Keep knees stacked.
  4. Dead Bug (60 sec): Lie on back, arms and legs in tabletop. Extend opposite arm and leg. Keep lower back pressed into mat.
  5. Supine Twist (45 sec each side): Lie on back, knees bent. Drop knees to one side. Keep shoulders flat.
⏱ 5 min — do daily, especially on training days

Conclusion: You Can Do BJJ for Life With a Bad Back

I've been training BJJ with a herniated disc for over two years. I've had days where I couldn't tie my belt without pain. I've tapped to things that felt ridiculous. I've told training partners "I can't roll today" more times than I can count.

But I'm still here. I'm still rolling. I'm still getting better. And so can you.

The key is not to fight through the pain — it's to train around it. These 7 rules aren't a restriction. They're a framework that allows you to keep doing what you love without destroying your body in the process. Listen to your back, adapt, and keep showing up.
