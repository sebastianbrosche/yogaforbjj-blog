My Story: How It Happened

It wasn't a dramatic moment. No screaming back, no pop, no collapse on the mat. It was a Tuesday night no-gi class, and I was playing deep half guard — one of my favorite positions at the time. My training partner drove forward to flatten me out, and I felt a familiar but sharper-than-usual ache in my lower left back. I finished the round, drove home, and woke up the next morning unable to stand up straight.

The MRI showed a 6mm herniated disc at L4-L5 with impingement on the left nerve root. The neurosurgeon's words are burned into my memory: "You need to stop grappling. At your age (41), these things don't heal well." He recommended surgery.

I didn't take his advice. Not because I'm reckless — but because I knew there had to be a better way. I'd been practicing yoga since 2011. I understood the body. I knew that if I could figure out how to train safely, I could keep doing the sport I loved.

Two years later, I'm still training. I'm a purple belt. I roll 4-5 times a week. My back isn't perfect — it never will be — but it's functional, it's strong, and it hasn't gotten worse. Here's exactly what I did.

"The surgeon sees a structural problem. The movement coach sees a compensation pattern. The BJJ player sees an opportunity to adapt."

The 3 Things I Stopped Doing

Before I tell you what worked, I need to tell you what I stopped doing. Because most people with disc injuries try to stretch their way out of it — and that's exactly wrong.

1. I Stopped Toe Touching and Forward Folds

This is the hardest one for BJJ players to accept. We're told flexibility is good. We see everyone stretching their hamstrings before class. But for a herniated disc, forward flexion — especially loaded flexion — is the enemy. Bending forward compresses the anterior portion of the disc and forces the nucleus pulposus backward, right into the nerve root. I stopped every movement that required me to fold forward with straight legs. Completely.

2. I Stopped "Stretching It Out" When My Back Was Sore

When your back feels tight after training, the natural instinct is to stretch it. I stopped doing this. Why? Because the "tightness" in a herniated disc case is often your body's protective muscle spasm — your erectors are clamping down to stabilize an unstable joint. Stretching them makes the instability worse. Instead of stretching, I started doing isometric core work to support the spine.

3. I Stopped Doing Rapid, Explosive Warmups

BJJ warmups often include jumping jacks, burpees, forward rolls, and rapid spinal movements. I stopped all of them. My warmup became slow, controlled, and breath-led. If the class warmup was too intense, I'd do my own on the side. Coach understood once I explained why.

The 5 Things I Started Doing

Here's the stuff that actually made a difference. Not theoretical exercises from Instagram — these are the five things I've done every single day (or every training day) for two years.

1. Decompression Hangs

This is the single most impactful thing I've done for my herniated disc. I bought a simple doorway pull-up bar and I hang from it for 30-60 seconds every morning and before every BJJ class. Gravity creates traction in the lumbar spine, creating space between the vertebrae and allowing the disc material to return to a more central position.

💪 Decompression Hang
How to Do It Safely With a Herniated Disc
  1. Grab a pull-up bar with an overhand grip, hands shoulder-width apart.
  2. Let your feet hang — don't jump up. If the bar is too high, use a step stool to get into position.
  3. Relax your shoulders. Let your body weight do the work. You should feel a gentle pull through your entire spine.
  4. If your grip gives out before 30 seconds, use straps or just hang as long as you can.
  5. Breathe deeply. On each exhale, imagine your spine lengthening.
  6. Start with 20 seconds. Work up to 60 seconds over 2 weeks.
⏱ 30-60 sec — do morning and before BJJ

Pro tip: If your shoulders are too tight to hang comfortably, start with a dead hang from parallel bars (like dip bars) where your feet can touch the ground and you can control how much weight you're hanging. Even 50% of your body weight in traction helps.

2. Bird Dogs

The bird dog is the gold standard exercise for disc health. It trains your posterior chain and deep stabilizers without loading the spine. I do 10 reps per side, holding for 3 seconds each, every single day.

🐕 Core Stabilizer
Bird Dog — The Single Best Disc Exercise
  1. Start on hands and knees, hands under shoulders, knees under hips.
  2. Keep your spine neutral — don't let your belly sag or your back round.
  3. Simultaneously extend your right arm forward and left leg back.
  4. Keep your hips square to the floor. Don't rotate or tilt.
  5. Hold for 3 seconds with control. No wobbling.
  6. Return to start and switch sides.
  7. Do 10 reps per side. If you shake or lose form, reduce reps.
⏱ 3 min — daily

3. Dead Bugs

Dead bugs retrain your brain to stabilize your spine while your limbs move — which is exactly what BJJ requires. I do these every morning before I even get out of bed.

🪲 Core Anti-Extension
Dead Bug — Train Your Spine to Stay Still
  1. Lie on your back with arms extended toward the ceiling and knees bent at 90° (tabletop position).
  2. Press your lower back into the mat. This is your starting position — do not lose it.
  3. Slowly extend your right arm overhead and your left leg toward the floor simultaneously.
  4. Keep your lower back pressed into the mat the entire time.
  5. Return to center and switch sides.
  6. Do 8-10 reps per side. If your back lifts off the mat, reduce the range of motion.
⏱ 3 min — daily

4. Specific Yoga Poses

Not all yoga is good for a herniated disc. But a few specific poses are invaluable. I do these in the evenings:

  • Supported Bridge (5 breaths): Lie on back, knees bent, feet flat. Lift hips, place a block under your sacrum. This is a gentle inversion that decompresses the lower back.
  • Supine Twist with Bent Knees (45 sec each side): The version where knees stay stacked and you rotate only through the thoracic spine. No lumbar rotation.
  • Legs-Up-the-Wall (5 min): Lie on your back with legs resting against a wall. This passive inversion takes pressure off the discs.
  • Psoas Release (2 min each side): Lie on your back with a yoga block or massage ball under your psoas (between your belly button and hip crease). Breathe. This releases the constant tension BJJ puts on the hip flexors.

5. Inflammation Management

A herniated disc creates both mechanical pressure and chemical inflammation. Managing the inflammation is just as important as managing the mechanics. Here's what I do:

  • Ice immediately after training — 15 minutes on the lower back, always with a barrier.
  • Turmeric and ginger — I add both to my post-training smoothie. Not a cure, but every bit helps.
  • Sleep position — I sleep on my back with a pillow under my knees, or on my side with a pillow between my knees. Never on my stomach.
  • No sitting for more than 30 minutes — I set a timer. When it goes off, I stand, walk, or do a quick cat-cow.

How I Modified My Rolling

Exercise is half the battle. The other half is how you actually train. Here's my rolling philosophy with a herniated disc:

Positional Sparring Over Full Rolling

I almost never do open-mat style rolling where anything can happen. Instead, I do positional sparring: start from a specific position, roll for 3-4 minutes, reset. This allows me to control which positions I'm putting my spine through. If my back feels good, I might do a full round at the end. But 80% of my "rolling" is positional.

Flow Rolling

Flow rolling — where both partners agree to move at 40-60% intensity and focus on transitions rather than submissions — is my default. It maintains the movement patterns of BJJ without the explosive loads that aggravate the disc. I flow roll for the first 2-3 rounds of every session.

My Tap Philosophy

I tap to positions, not just submissions. If someone stacks me in half guard, I tap. If someone gets a body triangle, I tap. If I feel any sharp sensation in my lower back, I tap. This has saved me countless times. The ego hit of tapping to a stack is nothing compared to the reality of reherniating a disc.

"Tap to the position before it becomes an injury. Your ego will recover faster than your disc."

What to Avoid in the Gym

This section is important because well-meaning people will give you bad advice.

  • "Just stretch it out" — If someone tells you to stretch your lower back when it hurts, they don't understand disc injuries. Most back pain from BJJ is not a tight muscle — it's a disc under pressure. Stretching can make it worse.
  • "You need to strengthen your core" — This is actually true, but the way most people interpret it (crunches, sit-ups, leg raises) is exactly wrong. Those exercises increase intra-abdominal pressure in a way that loads the disc. Planks, dead bugs, and bird dogs are the right core work. Crunches are the enemy.
  • "Just take a break for a few weeks" — Rest alone doesn't fix a herniated disc. The disc needs movement (within a pain-free range) to get nutrients. Complete rest leads to muscle atrophy and stiffness, which makes the problem worse. Don't stop moving — just move intelligently.
  • "Get surgery, it's the only option" — Surgery has its place, but it's not the first option. The vast majority of herniated discs resolve with conservative management within 6-12 months. Get a second opinion before going under the knife.

The Recovery Timeline

Everyone's timeline is different. But here's what mine looked like, to give you a realistic expectation:

Month 1: The Dark Days

I couldn't train at all. Walking was painful. Sitting was painful. I did 5-minute walks, cat-cow, and diaphragmatic breathing. I thought my BJJ career was over.

Month 3: First Steps Back

I started going to class but only watching. Then drilling techniques with trusted partners at 20% intensity. No rolling. I did decompression hangs and bird dogs every day.

Month 6: Flow Rolling

I started positional flow rolling with my safest training partner. I tapped early and often. I discovered that top positions (mount, side control) felt better than bottom positions. I started playing more top game.

Month 9: Regular Training (Modified)

I could roll at 60-70% intensity with most partners. I still avoided certain positions and tapped to back attacks immediately. My back felt stable most of the time. The daily exercises became a non-negotiable habit.

Year 1: Almost Normal

I was rolling 4 times a week. I competed in a local tournament (and lost, but that's not the point). My back still had occasional flare-ups, but I knew how to manage them. The fear of re-injury was fading.

Year 2: The New Normal

This is where I am now. I train 4-5 times a week. I still do my morning routine, my pre-class decompression hangs, and my post-class cooldown. I still tap to positions that feel dangerous. My back isn't the same as before the injury — but it's strong, it's stable, and it allows me to do what I love.

Can You Build Muscle With a Herniated Disc?

Yes. But you have to be smart about it.

Traditional deadlifts and heavy squats are probably off the table (they are for me). But you can build plenty of strength with:

  • Trap bar deadlifts — The trap bar shifts the load more centrally and reduces shear force on the spine compared to a straight bar. Start light.
  • Kettlebell swings — The hip hinge pattern strengthens the posterior chain without loading the spine — if you maintain a neutral spine. Do not round your back.
  • Single-leg work — Bulgarian split squats, lunges, and step-ups build leg strength without spinal compression.
  • Pull-ups and rows — Upper body pulling is generally safe and builds the lats, which help stabilize the lower back.
  • Pallof presses — Anti-rotation core work builds the obliques and deep stabilizers.

The key principle: never load a rounded spine. If you can maintain a neutral spine throughout the movement, you can build muscle safely. If your form breaks at any point, reduce the weight.

Your BJJ Career Isn't Over

A herniated disc diagnosis feels like a death sentence for your BJJ journey. I know. I've been there. The fear, the frustration, the imposter syndrome when you step back on the mat — I've felt all of it.

But here's the truth: your BJJ career isn't over because of a disc injury. It's just beginning differently.

You'll develop better body awareness. You'll become a more technical grappler because you can't rely on explosive, athletic movements. You'll appreciate every pain-free training session in a way you never did before. And the discipline of daily rehab will make you stronger — not just physically, but mentally.

I still have bad days. Days where my back feels like glass and I have to skip training. But those days are fewer and farther between. And every time I tie my belt, I'm grateful that I didn't listen to the surgeon who told me to quit.

Keep showing up. Adapt. And trust the process.
