The BJJ Athlete's Recovery Blueprint (PEARS)

Sebastian Brosche 2026-09-13 12 min read

You are not a pro fighter. If you train BJJ more than three times a week, you still load like one. Work, rush to the gym, every roll you can get, then emails or kids. Next day you are stiff as a rock and you call that the price of getting good.

It is not. Stiffness and Friday rounds that feel underwater are what happen when recovery is optional. This is the PEARS ebook as a pillar: the recovery principles I actually use, without ice-bath theatre.

1. Sleep is the king

You can drill, lift, stretch, and eat clean. If you are not sleeping enough, you are holding back healing, learning, and the ability to show up again.

Sleep is when tissue repairs, hormones settle, and the brain files the class you just did. REM, which does a lot of the memory and mood work, piles up in the last hours. That is why six hours is not a personality. It is a cut of the useful part.

The most dangerous idea is "I do fine on five hours." You adapt enough to function. You do not thrive. The phone in the bedroom is the modern version of leaving the lights on: blue light, dopamine, 45 minutes gone.

Practical, from the ebook, no gadget required:

  • Same sleep and wake time, including weekends, as close as you can
  • Room cool, dark, phone actually out of the room
  • Caffeine cut after mid-afternoon; alcohol is a sleep wrecker even at one glass
  • Dim lights in the last hour

I am not going to paste a scare-list of disease statistics here. Sleep loss is bad enough without turning this into a medical blog. Protect it like you protect your neck.

2. Active recovery, not a lighter version of BJJ

Passive rest (couch, skip, hope) slows things down. It does not move you forward. You come back still stiff.

The other trap is "recovery drilling": the same patterns at lower volume. Swimmers swim easy. Grapplers flow instead of spar. You stayed in the specialist box.

Active recovery needs contrast. Mobility is the best tool I have for that. Full joint range, no extra fatigue if you keep it honest, 10 to 15 minutes. Hike, swim, easy bike if you like. Do not recover from rolling by only rolling slower.

3. Nutrition as the internal environment

Inflammation after training is part of adaptation. Chronic inflammation from ultra-processed food, sugar, and seed-oil sludge is the saboteur. Whole plants, legumes, seeds, herbs: boring and effective. Protein for repair: athletes need more than the poster on the gym wall. ISSN range cited in the ebook is 1.6-2.2 g per kg, spread through the day, not one heroic chicken breast at 10pm.^1

One grocery swap at a time. Same rule as Do BJJ for Life.

4. Mobility after class (the hour that decides tomorrow)

The first hour after training is when you either start recovering or you drive home clenched. Five to fifteen minutes of slow stretching, long exhales, legs up the wall if the back is cooked.

Link: Stretching for jiu jitsu

5. Deep stretching, breath, NSDR

PEARS groups four modalities I want people to actually use:

  • Mobility: control through range
  • Deep stretching: long holds for the fascia and the "duct tape" feeling
  • Breathwork: downshift the nervous system after adrenaline rounds
  • NSDR: guided stillness, the reset you will not get from scrolling

You do not need all four every night. You need one on purpose.

6. Deload like an adult

Hobbyists copy competitor volume with a competitor's recovery budget they do not have. If you are getting more brittle, the answer is often one easier week, not a new supplement.

What this is not

It is not medical advice. It is not "yoga will fix a torn ligament." If something is sharp, unstable, or getting worse, see a human who can diagnose. Then use this for the 90 percent that is load management and habits.
